## Why Most Published Research Findings Are False

### Links

* https://journals.plos.org/plosmedicine/article/file?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pmed.0020124&type=printable

### Notes

#### Summary

Ioannidis presents a probabilistic argument showing why statistically significant findings can have a surprisingly low probability of being true. The reliability of a published claim depends not only on its p-value, but also on prior plausibility, statistical power, bias, flexibility in analysis, and how many hypotheses are being explored.

#### Key ideas

* A low p-value controls the false-positive rate **conditional on the null hypothesis**; it is not the probability that the published claim is false.
* The positive predictive value of a finding depends on how many tested relationships are actually true before the study begins.
* Small, underpowered studies are more likely to produce unstable estimates and less reliable positive findings.
* When true effects are small, distinguishing them from noise becomes harder.
* Trying many hypotheses, outcomes, subgroups, models, or analysis choices creates more opportunities to find significance by chance.
* Selective reporting, researcher bias, financial incentives, and competition can further increase the share of misleading findings.
* Replication, larger samples, preregistration, transparent analysis, and stronger prior evidence improve reliability.

#### Important nuance

The title is intentionally provocative. The paper develops a mathematical framework under explicit assumptions; it is not an empirical census proving that more than half of every scientific literature is false.

#### Takeaway

Statistical significance is only one piece of evidence. To judge whether a result is credible, also ask about prior plausibility, power, multiplicity, researcher flexibility, bias, and independent replication.
